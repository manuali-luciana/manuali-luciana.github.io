I'm a PhD Candidate in Economics at the IDEA Graduate Program, jointly run by the Institut d'Anàlisi Econòmica (IAE-CSIC), the Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona, and the Barcelona School of Economics. My advisors are Cristina Bellés-Obrero and Lídia Farré.

My research focuses on applied microeconomics, with a special interest in labor economics, gender economics, and development economics.

Before my PhD, I completed a Master in Economics and Finance at CEMFI and my undergraduate degree at the Universidad Nacional de Córdoba, Argentina.

Working papers

Criminalizing Digital Sexual Violence: Evidence from Mexico's Ley Olimpia

The first causal evidence that criminalizing digital sexual violence reduces it.

Digital sexual violence, including the non-consensual sharing of intimate images, online harassment, and deepfakes, has emerged as a pervasive form of gender-based violence, yet legal frameworks have lagged behind technological change. This paper provides the first causal evaluation of a law criminalizing digital sexual violence. I exploit the staggered adoption of Mexico's Ley Olimpia across its 32 states between 2018 and 2021, using nationally representative microdata from INEGI's Módulo sobre Ciberacoso (MOCIBA). Employing a staggered difference-in-differences design, I find that the law reduced the non-consensual online sharing of personal or intimate content by 3.8 percentage points among women aged 18 to 24, roughly a 60 percent reduction relative to the counterfactual level victimization would have reached for treated cohorts absent the law. Event-study estimates reveal no pre-trends and immediate effects upon adoption. I interpret the results through a two-stage framework: an awareness stage, in which adoption raises public information about the new offense, and a behavioral stage, in which both potential victims and potential perpetrators respond. Consistent with this framework, Google search interest for the law peaks around adoption and remains elevated thereafter, self-assessed information protection rises among young women, and the reduction in victimization is larger in states that adopted higher penalties. These findings suggest that targeted criminalization can meaningfully curb technology-facilitated abuse.

The Impact of Raising Labor Standards for Domestic Workers

Formalizing contracts on paper did not change the working day.

This paper examines how Spain's Royal Decree 1620/2011, a sector-specific labor reform targeting domestic workers, affected their job quality and employment outcomes. Using a difference-in-differences design, I compare women employed in domestic service with women working in low-wage service activities not directly affected by the reform. The analysis draws on quarterly microdata from Spain's labor-force survey (Encuesta de Población Activa, EPA) from 2008 to 2015. I find that the reform generated substantial contractual formalization: the share of domestic workers without a written contract fell by about 8 percentage points, a 38 percent reduction relative to baseline. The probability of holding an indefinite contract rose by about 3 percentage points. Employment declined by only about 1 percentage point, indicating minimal adjustment in overall labor demand. However, measures related to working time changed very little. Average weekly hours and total hours worked remained essentially unchanged, and unpaid extra hours did not decline. The main adjustment occurred in how work was distributed across employers: the probability of holding more than one job increased by about 0.7 percentage points. Average tenure fell by roughly 21 months, consistent with long-standing informal relationships being replaced by new, more formal arrangements. These results highlight that sector-specific labor reforms can substantially improve contractual coverage without reducing employment, though effective working conditions within households may be slower to change.
